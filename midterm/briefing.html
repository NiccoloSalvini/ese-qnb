Midterm briefing — how to read the brief and how to write the report

Delivered in class, week 2 (Thursday 1 October). Due Sunday 1 November, 23:59, on Turnitin; presentations the week of 2 November.

The official brief lives on the student portal and is the only authority on what is asked. This page is the method: how to read it, in what order to work, and where marks are lost. It deliberately repeats none of the brief’s own content.

What is being asked of you

A research report for a Board, 1,500 words ±10%, built on the study of a firm’s cost, revenue and profit functions, plus qualitative sections on the business context. Then an oral presentation.

Written part 80%, oral 20%.

Two things are being marked at once: whether the mathematics is right, and whether a Board would act on what you wrote. Most people are stronger at the first than they think and weaker at the second.

How the paper is marked

Two layers, and the second one is a quarter of the mark.

weight
the content of the questions — relevance, depth, structure, the mathematics, each question at its own weight in the brief 75%
referencing — every claim from outside sourced, in-text, Harvard 15%
bibliography — complete, Harvard, sources a sceptic would accept 5%
language — precise, the vocabulary of the course 5%

A perfect study of the functions with no sources is capped at three quarters of the mark before anyone reads a line. A correct reference list costs an afternoon; do it properly.

The oral is marked on the quality of the work presented (30%), the use of course material (30%), its value to the audience — would a Board act on it? (30%), and delivery (10%).

The order to work in

Not the order the report is read in.

  1. The mathematics first, on paper. Every function studied in full, every number checked twice. Until this is done you have nothing to write about.
  2. The sketches. By hand, axes labelled, every key point marked. Do them once the study is finished, never before.
  3. The findings section. Turn each step of the study into a sentence about the firm.
  4. The qualitative sections. These take longer than you expect because they need sources.
  5. The executive summary, last. It summarises a report that now exists.
  6. The front matter — cover, contents — and the reference list.

The seven elements, in the brief’s own words

Every function you are asked about gets all seven, in this order, labelled:

The brief names them. Use its words, in its order, as your headings — a marker looking for seven things should find seven headings.

1 domain — as an interval, with the bracket chosen on purpose
2 limits — at the ends of the domain and at any break; name the asymptotes
3 range — the values the function actually takes
4 sign — where positive, where negative; the zeros are how you find out
5 maxima and minima — f'(q) = 0 for the candidates, the sign of f'' for the verdict
6 convexity and inflection points — where f'' changes sign
7 sketch — labelled with everything above

Range and limits are the two most often skipped, and the brief asks for both by name. Omit the sign of f'' and you have not answered the question either, however good the picture is.

Writing the findings so a Board can use them

Each mathematical result has a business sentence attached. Write both.

You found What you write
q* = 150 “The firm should produce 150 units a month.”
π(150) = 30,000 “At that output it earns €30,000 a month.”
roots at 63.4 and 236.6 “It makes money between 64 and 236 units; outside that window it loses.”
π''< 0 “That output is a genuine maximum, not a turning point of another kind.”

The Board reads the right-hand column. The marker checks that the left-hand column earns it.

The qualitative sections

They are not filler and they are not marked as prose. A claim about the firm’s context needs a source, cited Harvard style, and the source needs to be one a sceptical reader would accept.

  • Two good sources used properly beat eight listed and unread.
  • Cite what you actually consulted. A reference list longer than your reading is the easiest kind of dishonesty to spot.
  • Connect the context to the mathematics. A section on market conditions that never touches the cost or revenue function has not done its job.

The questions about the world outside the firm

Two kinds of question in a brief like this one are not calculus, and they are worth as much as a function study. They are research and critical analysis: there is no reading list, because finding, choosing and judging the sources is part of what is marked.

  • Risks from outside, and protectionism. For every point: what it is, precisely; recent, reliable evidence, cited Harvard; the channel to the firm — which term of the cost or revenue function it moves; the direction and, where your source allows, the size; and what the Board should do. Week-2 slides, The questions with no formula (added 22 October).
  • A tariff on an input — the firm. This part is quantitative: say which term moves (the per-unit cost of what is imported) and which do not (the plant’s internal costs, the fixed cost), rewrite the function, and re-solve — new best output, new profit, new break-evens, by bisection where algebra cannot reach. Addendum at the end of the week-3 slides, block C; drills D11–D12.
  • The country. Block D of the same addendum shows one way to organise the argument (who gains, who pays, what is lost). It is optional: the question is marked on the quality of the analysis and the sources, not on that model.

Where marks are lost, in order of frequency

  1. A sketch instead of a study. Seven steps, or the question is unanswered.
  2. No second derivative. “It is a maximum because the graph looks like a hill” is worth nothing.
  3. The arithmetic of clearing a fraction. Multiplying through by (q+1) under pressure is where good candidates slip. Check by substituting back.
  4. Numbers with no sentence. A page of correct calculus and no statement of what the firm should do.
  5. Qualitative sections written last and fast, with references bolted on.
  6. Submitting on Sunday evening. Turnitin queues, and there is no late submission for postgraduate work. A missed deadline means one resubmission capped at 50%.

The oral

Eight to fifteen minutes, slides, in the week of 2 November.

You will be asked at least one unscripted question about your own sketch. The way to prepare is not to memorise the presentation but to be able to explain, pointing at the picture, why the curve turns where it does.

Using AI on this

Permitted, and expected to be disclosed. Two rules make it safe:

  • Every line you submit, you can explain. If you cannot reconstruct a step at the board, it should not be in the report.
  • Check every number yourself. Assistants are fluent about calculus and wrong about arithmetic in ways that read perfectly.

The office hour

One optional online hour during reading week. Bring a draft, however rough. A blank page is a much harder conversation than a wrong one, and by then there is no time to start from nothing.
